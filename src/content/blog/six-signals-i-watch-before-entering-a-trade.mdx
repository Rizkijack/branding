Every trade I took without a checklist was a trade I was really taking on
emotion. Not always — sometimes the setup was genuinely clean — but the process
was mine, not the market's, and that is enough to lose money slowly.

This is the checklist. It is not exhaustive, it is not clever, and after two
years of revisions it fits on one screen.

## 1. Where is the liquidity?

Not "is there volume" — where is it, and is it real.

On-chain this is concrete: a few hundred thousand dollars of depth inside a
1% band means my exit is a haircut, not a market order. Pool depth that appeared
two hours ago and was seeded by one wallet is a liquidity mirage. If I cannot
name the pools or venues I am trading into, I do not have an edge, I have a
position.

On a central exchange the same question, different vocabulary: how thin is the
book between here and my stop?

> If I cannot exit without moving the price, I am not trading — I am donating.

## 2. Is the structure aligned?

Structure is boring on purpose. I want to know:

- Which timeframe am I trading on, and am I being honest about it?
- Where is the last swing high and the last swing low?
- Am I entering into a level that has already been respected twice, or one that
  is only respected in my imagination?

The failure mode here is not bad analysis. It is _unfalsifiable_ analysis — a
setup that would be invalidated by evidence but is never actually triggered,
because no evidence ever quite counts. When I catch myself unable to write down
the exact price that would prove me wrong, I skip the trade.

## 3. What is my invalidation, in price?

One number. Written before the entry, not after the loss.

This is the least glamorous item on the list and it is the one that saves the
account. If the invalidation is "if it reverts", that is not an invalidation —
that is a hope. If it is "below the swing low at 0.41", then the trade has a
shape and I can size it.

## 4. How big is the position, really?

Position size is the _only_ variable I fully control on any given trade. Not
the entry, not the exit, not the market. So it gets the attention.

I work in R-multiples rather than percentages, because it forces the arithmetic
that matters — how much am I losing if this is wrong, expressed in units of the
risk I already accepted.

```python title="sizing.py" showLineNumbers
def size_position(
    equity: float,
    risk_pct: float,
    entry: float,
    invalidation: float,
    fee_pct: float = 0.001,
    buffer_pct: float = 0.25,
) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Return (position_units, risk_cash). `invalidation` must be on the
    losing side of `entry`; we widen the stop by `buffer_pct` to survive
    spread and wick."""
    if (invalidation - entry) * (1 if entry > invalidation else -1) <= 0:
        raise ValueError("invalidation is not on the losing side of entry")

    risk_cash = equity * (risk_pct / 100)
    stop_distance = abs(entry - invalidation) * (1 + buffer_pct / 100)
    units = risk_cash / (stop_distance * (1 + fee_pct))
    return units, risk_cash
```

The buffer is doing real work. A stop placed exactly at the swing low gets
swept and then recovers, which is functionally identical to having no stop when
you measure performance by trade count.

| Risk per trade | Expectancy needed to break even | What it feels like                      |
| -------------- | ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| 0.5%           | Very high                       | Hard to notice, easy to honour          |
| 1%             | Moderate                        | Sustainable over months                 |
| 2%             | Low                             | Survivable, but variance starts showing |
| 5%             | Almost none                     | One bad week ends the run               |

The numbers are not advice — they are a reminder that risk per trade is a
_choice about variance_, not a measure of confidence. Confidence is exactly the
thing I am least qualified to assess about my own trade.

## 5. Does the setup survive being wrong?

Before I enter, I write the single most likely reason this fails. Then I check
whether that reason is already priced in.

Most of the time it is not. That is the trade. Occasionally it _is_ priced,
and then the correct action is to pass, even though everything else lined up.
FOMO is not a signal; it is a feeling with a very poor hit rate.

## 6. Can I walk away?

The last one is about me, not the market.

Is there a level where I take the whole thing off regardless of how it looks?
Can I close the chart and come back to it in four hours without refreshing?
Do I have something else scheduled that pulls my attention away?

A setup that requires me to watch a candle-by-candle move is a setup I cannot
size honestly, because my attention is now a position risk I never priced.

## Why this list exists

I wrote it down after losing a month of gains to three trades that each would
have been skipped by the list above. Not by much — each one only needed two
items to fail. But they were correlated, in the same direction, on the same
day, which is the part that actually hurt.

Trading is the only thing I do where I can be wrong for a long time and still
call it "the process". That makes process-building the entire game, and the
checklist is just the process written down where I cannot quietly edit it.

Boring checklists survive. Gut feel, in my experience, does not.
